# The Distribution Workforce Crisis: By the Numbers

![Workforce crisis data visualization showing key statistics: 25% of workforce 55+, 26.7% turnover, 2.1M unfilled jobs by 2030](/media/posts/distribution-workforce-crisis/01-hero.svg)

Industrial distribution runs on people. Counterpeople who know that "the brass thing that goes on the water heater" means a 3/4" FIP x 3/4" FIP stainless steel braided connector. Inside sales reps who can quote a 200-line plumbing rough-in from a set of plans. Warehouse staff who know that the Watts 3/4" PRV is in aisle 7, bay 3, even though the system says aisle 12.

Those people are leaving faster than the industry can replace them. And the data says it's going to get worse before it gets better.

This is not a speculative piece about future risks. These are the numbers — sourced, current, and pointing in the same direction. The distribution workforce crisis is here, it's measurable, and it's already affecting service quality at branches across the country.

## The Numbers

### An Aging Workforce

**25% of the manufacturing and distribution workforce is age 55 or older**, according to the [Manufacturing Institute](https://www.themanufacturinginstitute.org/). That means one in four workers is within a decade of retirement — and many are closer than that.

In distribution specifically, the concentration at the senior end is even more pronounced in customer-facing roles. The most experienced counterpeople — the ones who carry the most product knowledge — tend to be the oldest. They started in the trade when they were young, built expertise over decades, and now represent an irreplaceable layer of institutional knowledge.

When these workers retire, they don't leave behind a manual. They leave behind a gap that takes years to fill — if it can be filled at all.

### Turnover That Won't Slow Down

**Voluntary turnover in wholesale and retail distribution reached 26.7% in 2025**, according to [Mercer's 2025 workforce survey](https://www.mercer.com/en-us/insights/people-strategy/workforce-trends/). That means more than one in four employees voluntarily left their jobs last year.

To put that in context: a distributor with 100 employees is replacing 27 of them every year. Each replacement carries a [training cost of $25,000-$40,000](/blog/cost-to-train-counterperson) when you account for all the hidden expenses — supervisor time, reduced productivity, errors during ramp-up. At $34,000 per hire (the midpoint estimate), that 100-person distributor is spending $918,000 annually just on turnover-related costs.

And turnover compounds. When experienced employees leave, the remaining staff absorb their workload, leading to burnout, which leads to more departures. It's a cycle that's difficult to break with hiring alone.

### The Unfilled Jobs Projection

**2.1 million manufacturing and distribution jobs are projected to go unfilled by 2030**, according to [Bureau of Labor Statistics projections](https://www.bls.gov/). This isn't a prediction that demand will create 2.1 million new positions. It's a projection that existing and anticipated positions will go unfilled because there aren't enough qualified workers to fill them.

The gap is driven by three converging forces:

1. **Retirements accelerating.** Baby boomers who delayed retirement during and after the pandemic are now leaving in larger numbers.
2. **Fewer entrants.** Younger workers are choosing technology, healthcare, and service industries over manufacturing and distribution.
3. **Skills mismatch.** The workers who are available often lack the trade-specific knowledge that distribution jobs require.

### The Onboarding Cost Multiplier

**The average cost to onboard a new employee is $4,100**, according to [SHRM](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/average-cost-per-hire-rises-to-4700-shrm-finds). But in distribution, that number is deceptively low. It covers HR processing, paperwork, and basic orientation — not the months of supervised training required before a new counterperson can independently identify the right part from a contractor's vague description.

The true cost, as we've detailed in our [training cost breakdown](/blog/cost-to-train-counterperson), is $25,000-$40,000 per hire when you account for supervisor time, productivity loss, and error costs.

Multiply that by an industry with 26.7% turnover, and you begin to see why workforce costs are consuming an outsized share of distributor operating budgets.

## The Knowledge Drain

The workforce crisis in distribution isn't just about headcount. It's about knowledge.

![Age distribution chart showing the concentration of experienced workers approaching retirement age versus younger workers entering the distribution workforce](/media/posts/distribution-workforce-crisis/02-demographic-shift.svg)

An experienced counterperson carries product knowledge that took decades to accumulate. They know:

- **Cross-references by memory.** Which Watts product is equivalent to which Honeywell product, and which one actually fits in the space available.
- **Trade language translations.** That "the gray stuff" means CPVC cement, that "a reducing tee" at this branch means something different than at the branch across town because local contractors use the term differently.
- **Application context.** Which fittings are code-compliant for potable water in this jurisdiction, which ones aren't, and which ones technically are but nobody uses because they fail in three years.
- **Customer patterns.** That when Johnson Mechanical orders 2" copper, they always need the long-radius elbows, even if they forget to specify.

None of this is in the catalog. None of this is in the training manual. It lives in the heads of people who are retiring.

When a 30-year veteran retires, the branch doesn't just lose a body — it loses a reference library. The new hire who replaces them might have enthusiasm and aptitude, but they don't have 30 years of pattern recognition. And the timeline to rebuild that knowledge is measured in years, not weeks.

## Regional Variations

The workforce crisis doesn't hit evenly. Several factors create regional hotspots where the problem is especially acute.

**Sun Belt growth markets** (Texas, Florida, Arizona, the Carolinas) face a double squeeze: construction demand is surging, driving up the need for distribution workers, while competition from other industries for the same labor pool is intense.

**Rural and semi-rural markets** have smaller labor pools to begin with. A distributor in a mid-size market might have a handful of candidates for a counterperson role, compared to dozens in a major metro. When their senior person retires, the replacement pipeline is shallow.

**High cost-of-living markets** (Northeast, Pacific Coast) struggle to offer wages competitive with other industries. A counterperson making $22/hour is competing against logistics, tech support, and retail management roles that pay more for less specialized knowledge.

**Rust Belt markets** have an aging workforce skewed older than the national average, meaning the retirement wave will hit these regions earlier and harder.

## The Impact on Service Quality

The workforce crisis isn't abstract. It shows up in daily operations.

**Longer hold times.** When branches are short-staffed, customers wait. Contractors on job sites don't wait — they call the next distributor.

**Higher error rates.** Newer employees make more mistakes. Wrong parts get shipped, returns increase, and re-delivery costs eat into already thin margins.

**Lost cross-sell opportunities.** An experienced counterperson knows that when a contractor orders a water heater, they'll also need a connector kit, an expansion tank, and probably a new PRV. A new hire processes the water heater order and nothing else.

**Customer defection.** Contractors are loyal to people, not brands. When their go-to counterperson leaves, they often follow — or they start splitting orders across multiple distributors, which means lower share of wallet for everyone.

## What Distributors Are Doing About It

The distributors that are managing through this crisis — not solving it, but managing it — are typically pursuing some combination of five strategies.

### 1. Compensation Adjustments

Raising wages is the most direct response, and many distributors have done it. Average counterperson wages have risen 12-18% over the past three years in competitive markets. But wages alone don't solve the problem. Distributors can't out-pay every industry competing for the same workers, and higher wages don't accelerate the knowledge-building timeline.

### 2. Culture and Career Pathing

Some distributors are investing in workplace culture, career advancement paths, and benefits packages designed to improve retention. This helps with the turnover rate but doesn't address the knowledge drain from retirements.

### 3. Technology That Reduces the Knowledge Burden

This is where [AI copilots](/blog/ai-copilot-industrial-distribution) enter the picture. Instead of requiring new hires to memorize 200,000 SKUs and thousands of cross-references, AI-assisted tools let them search the catalog using natural language — the same way a customer describes what they need.

A new hire doesn't need to know that the brass fitting the customer described is a SharkBite 3/4" x 1/2" push-to-connect transition coupling. They need to type the customer's description into a tool that returns the right part number. The AI handles the translation from trade language to SKU.

This doesn't replace product knowledge — experienced people will always be more effective. But it dramatically shortens the period where a new hire is essentially useless without supervision.

### 4. Knowledge Capture Programs

Forward-thinking distributors are [building knowledge bases](/blog/knowledge-base-distribution) that capture institutional expertise before it retires. The most effective approach isn't asking veterans to write down everything they know (they won't, and they can't — most knowledge is tacit). It's implementing systems that capture knowledge as a byproduct of daily work.

When an experienced counterperson uses an AI copilot and corrects its suggestion — "No, for that application you want the Watts LF25AUB, not the standard 25AUB" — that correction becomes part of the system's knowledge. Over time, the AI accumulates the expert's judgment without requiring them to sit down and document it.

### 5. Recruiting Beyond Traditional Channels

Some distributors are expanding their recruiting beyond trade backgrounds. If AI tools can bridge the product knowledge gap, the hiring criteria shifts from "must know plumbing products" to "must be good with customers and technology." That opens the candidate pool significantly.

## The Role of Technology

Technology alone will not solve the distribution workforce crisis. There is no AI that replaces a 30-year counterperson's relationship with a contractor who's been buying from them since the Clinton administration. But technology can do three things that matter:

**Make new employees productive faster.** AI copilots for [part identification](/blog/how-ai-part-identification-works) compress the training timeline from months to weeks for basic competency. That doesn't create experts overnight, but it creates functional employees who can handle the majority of orders without constant supervision.

**Preserve knowledge that would otherwise be lost.** Every query processed, every correction made, every cross-reference confirmed — these build a knowledge base that persists after the expert retires. The [state of AI in distribution](/blog/state-of-ai-industrial-distribution-2026) shows this as one of the fastest-growing application areas.

**Make the job more attractive.** Younger workers expect modern tools. A branch that gives new hires a green-screen terminal and a 600-page catalog is at a recruiting disadvantage compared to one that provides AI-assisted search and modern interfaces.

None of this is a silver bullet. The numbers are stark, and the demographic trends are baked in. But distributors that invest in tools and processes that multiply the effectiveness of the people they have — rather than hoping to hire their way out of the problem — are positioned to weather the crisis better than those that don't.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How bad is the labor shortage in industrial distribution?

The numbers paint a severe picture: 25% of the manufacturing and distribution workforce is age 55 or older (Manufacturing Institute), voluntary turnover in wholesale and retail hit 26.7% in 2025 (Mercer), and the BLS projects 2.1 million unfilled manufacturing and distribution jobs by 2030. The problem is both a headcount shortage and a knowledge drain.

### Why is distribution workforce turnover so high?

Multiple factors drive 26.7% voluntary turnover: physically demanding work, competition from other industries offering better pay and benefits, perception issues (distribution isn't seen as a "career" by younger workers), limited advancement paths at many distributors, and inadequate tools that make the job harder than it needs to be.

### What happens when experienced distributors retire?

When a 30-year counterperson retires, decades of product knowledge, cross-reference expertise, customer relationships, and trade shortcuts leave with them. This institutional knowledge — which products substitute for which, what contractors in the area typically need, which manufacturer specs are actually reliable — is rarely documented and extremely expensive to rebuild.

### How are distributors addressing the workforce shortage?

Leading distributors are using a combination of strategies: competitive compensation adjustments, improved workplace culture, technology investments that reduce the knowledge burden on new hires, AI-assisted tools for part identification and cross-referencing, and programs to capture institutional knowledge from experienced employees before they retire.

### Can technology solve the distribution labor shortage?

Technology can't solve the headcount problem directly, but it can significantly reduce the impact. AI copilots make new employees productive faster, knowledge management tools preserve expertise that would otherwise be lost, and modern interfaces make distribution jobs more attractive to younger workers. The goal isn't to replace people — it's to make fewer people more effective.

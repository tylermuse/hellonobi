# How Distributors Can Speed Up Counter Service During Morning Rush

If you have ever worked in a distribution branch, you know the feeling. It is 6:15 in the morning. The parking lot is already filling up. Contractors are lined up at the counter, some with detailed lists, others with a vague idea of what they need. Everyone has a job site to get to. Nobody wants to wait.

The morning rush is the most important 90 minutes of the day for most distribution branches. It is when you serve the most customers, process the most transactions, and — if things go wrong — lose the most business. Every minute a contractor spends waiting at your counter is a minute they are not on the job. And it is a minute they are thinking about whether the competitor down the street would be faster.

Speed at the counter is not a nice-to-have. It is a competitive requirement.

## The Anatomy of the Morning Rush

The pattern is predictable across the industry. Contractors arrive between 6:00 and 7:30am, with the peak hitting around 6:30. They need materials for the day's work. Some have planned ahead with purchase orders or will-call requests. Many have not.

According to [Supply House Times](https://www.supplyht.com/), the counter experience remains the primary touchpoint for contractor relationships in distribution, with speed and product knowledge ranking as the top two factors in customer satisfaction — ahead of pricing in most surveys.

![Morning rush timeline](/media/posts/speed-up-counter-service/01-hero.svg)

During a typical morning rush, a busy branch might process 30 to 50 transactions between 6:00 and 7:30am with three to four counterpeople working. That is roughly one transaction every three to four minutes per person. When a single transaction takes longer than expected — a complex lookup, a cross-reference question, an inventory check — the line backs up fast.

The math is unforgiving. If your average transaction takes four minutes and you have three counterpeople, you can serve about 45 customers per hour. If average transaction time creeps up to six minutes because of slow product lookups, that drops to 30 customers per hour. That is 15 contractors per hour who are either waiting in a longer line or walking out the door.

## What Actually Slows Things Down

When you break down a typical counter transaction, the time sinks become clear. Most of the operational steps — scanning items, processing payment, printing invoices — are fast and consistent. The variability, and the bottleneck, almost always comes from the same place.

### Product Lookups

This is the big one. A contractor walks up and says, "I need a two-inch copper press coupling — do you have the Viega or something equivalent?" If the counterperson knows the answer from memory, this takes ten seconds. If they do not, it can take three to five minutes of searching catalogs, navigating the ERP system, or walking to the back to find the product.

The gap between an experienced counterperson who knows the catalog and a newer employee who does not can be enormous. A veteran handles that Viega request in seconds. A new hire might spend five minutes hunting through the system, trying different search terms, and still not be confident they found the right part.

### Cross-Referencing

Contractors often ask for a specific manufacturer but are open to alternatives. "Do you have the Watts version of this?" or "What crosses to that Moen cartridge?" Cross-referencing requires deep product knowledge that takes years to develop. When a counterperson cannot cross-reference on the spot, they either lose the sale or hold up the line while they figure it out.

### Availability Checks

When the first-choice product is not on the shelf, the counterperson needs to check whether it is in the warehouse, available at another branch, or on order. This often means navigating multiple screens in the ERP, making phone calls, or physically walking to the warehouse. Each of these takes time.

### New Employee Hesitation

Every branch has experienced this: a newer counterperson gets a request they are not confident about. They hesitate. They ask a colleague. The colleague is serving their own customer. Now two transactions are slowed down. As we explored in our article on [training new counterpeople faster](/blog/train-counterpeople-faster), this hesitation is the practical consequence of the product knowledge gap.

## Strategies That Work: The Operational Side

Before we talk about technology, there are proven operational strategies that distributors use to manage the morning rush. These are worth implementing regardless of what tools you deploy.

### Pre-Staged Will-Call Orders

The single most effective way to speed up morning rush is to shift work to off-peak hours. Will-call programs let contractors phone in or submit orders the evening before. Your team picks and stages the materials overnight or in the early morning. When the contractor arrives, they check in, sign, and go.

Well-run will-call programs can handle 30 to 40 percent of morning transactions, dramatically reducing counter congestion. According to [Distribution Strategy Group](https://www.distributionstrategy.com/), the most operationally efficient branches have invested heavily in will-call and pre-order capabilities, treating the counter as the last resort rather than the default.

The key is making it easy for contractors to submit orders. A phone call to a dedicated line, a simple web form, or even a text message system can work. The lower the friction, the higher the adoption.

### Dedicated Counter Lanes

Some larger branches implement a fast-lane system, similar to grocery store express checkouts. One lane handles quick picks — customers who know exactly what they need and just want to grab it and go. Another lane handles complex orders, custom configurations, and customers who need consultation.

This prevents a single complex transaction from holding up five contractors who just need to pick up a box of fittings. It requires enough counter staff to dedicate a lane, which is not always feasible for smaller branches, but it makes a meaningful difference where it can be implemented.

### Digital Order-Ahead

More distributors are investing in digital tools that let contractors build orders on their phone or computer before arriving at the branch. This is different from traditional e-commerce — it is specifically designed for the contractor workflow, where the customer still wants to pick up at the branch but does not want to wait in line.

[MDM (Modern Distribution Management)](https://www.mdm.com/) has reported on the growing adoption of mobile ordering tools in distribution, noting that contractors increasingly expect the same convenience they get from consumer apps. The branches that offer a smooth order-ahead experience are seeing faster counter throughput and higher customer satisfaction.

### Warehouse Layout and Counter Proximity

Physical layout matters more than many branch managers realize. If your most frequently ordered products are at the far end of the warehouse, every transaction that requires a warehouse pull adds minutes of walking time. Locating high-velocity SKUs near the counter, maintaining a well-organized will-call staging area, and ensuring counter staff can verify stock without leaving their station all contribute to speed.

## The AI Copilot Advantage: Instant Lookups

Operational strategies are essential, but they do not solve the fundamental bottleneck: the time it takes to look up a product when the counterperson does not know the answer from memory.

This is where an AI copilot has the biggest impact on counter speed.

### Every Counterperson Becomes Your Fastest Counterperson

An AI copilot lets any counterperson — from a 20-year veteran to a hire who started last week — find products at the same speed. The contractor describes what they need in natural language. The copilot searches the distributor's own catalog, interprets trade terminology, and returns the right SKU.

"Two-inch copper press coupling, Viega or equivalent" gets an instant answer. No catalog navigation. No hunting through the ERP. No asking a colleague.

For a branch with a mix of experienced and newer counterpeople, this levels the playing field during the rush. Your newest hire is not the bottleneck anymore. They are serving customers at the same pace as your veterans.

### Cross-References in Seconds

The AI copilot handles manufacturer cross-references automatically. When a contractor asks for a brand you do not carry, the copilot immediately surfaces equivalents from the brands you do stock. This eliminates one of the most time-consuming aspects of counter service and turns potential lost sales into completed transactions.

### Confidence Eliminates Hesitation

When a counterperson trusts that they can find any product quickly, they stop hesitating. They engage the customer immediately. They do not freeze when they hear an unfamiliar part number or manufacturer name. That confidence translates directly into faster service.

As we discussed in our piece on [managing product knowledge after an acquisition](/blog/product-knowledge-after-acquisition), this confidence factor becomes even more critical when branches are handling expanded product lines that staff has not had time to learn through experience.

## Measuring the Impact

The metrics that matter for counter speed are straightforward:

**Average transaction time.** Track this during peak hours specifically, not as a daily average. A branch that reduces peak-hour average transaction time from five minutes to three minutes can serve 60 percent more customers in the same window.

**Line length and wait time.** If you are not measuring this, start. Even informal tracking — noting the longest the line gets during morning rush — gives you a baseline. According to [Mercer's workforce research](https://www.mercer.com/), customer-facing efficiency metrics are increasingly tied to employee retention, as frustrated workers in high-pressure environments are more likely to leave.

**Walk-away rate.** This is the hardest to measure but the most important. How many contractors look at the line, decide it is too long, and leave? Some branches track this by reviewing security camera footage. Others survey contractors. The contractors who walk away are not just a lost transaction — they are a relationship at risk.

The distributors who treat counter speed as a measurable, improvable metric — and invest in both operational strategies and AI tools to move the needle — will win the morning rush. And in distribution, winning the morning rush is winning the day.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why is the morning rush so critical for distribution branches?

Most contractors arrive between 6:00 and 7:30am to pick up materials before heading to job sites. This 90-minute window can represent 40 to 50 percent of a branch's daily transactions. Long wait times during this period directly risk losing customers to competitors who can serve them faster.

### What is the biggest bottleneck during counter service?

Product lookups are consistently the single biggest time sink. When a contractor describes a part using trade language and the counterperson needs to search catalogs, cross-reference manufacturers, or ask a colleague, a transaction that should take 90 seconds can stretch to 5 or 10 minutes.

### How do will-call and order-ahead programs speed up counter service?

Will-call orders allow contractors to phone or submit orders in advance so materials are staged and ready for pickup. This shifts the product lookup and picking work to off-peak hours, freeing counter staff to focus on walk-in customers during rush periods.

### Can AI copilots reduce transaction times at the distribution counter?

Yes. AI copilots eliminate the product lookup bottleneck by allowing counterpeople to search the catalog using natural language. Instead of navigating complex catalog systems or waiting for a veteran employee, any counterperson can find the right SKU in seconds, regardless of their experience level.

### How does counter speed affect customer retention in distribution?

Contractors are time-sensitive buyers. If they consistently wait too long at your counter, they will shift their purchasing to a competitor. In most markets, there are multiple distributors within a short drive. Speed is one of the few differentiators that contractors consistently rank as a top priority.
